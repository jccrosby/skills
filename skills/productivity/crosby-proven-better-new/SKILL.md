# Evaluate an idea with Proven-Better-New

Research alternatives, assess the proposed value, and identify assumptions worth testing.
Support personal tools, internal projects, open-source projects, and commercial apps.
Use a direct, professional tone.
Do not activate this workflow merely because someone expresses excitement or asks for implementation help.

This skill adapts Mark Pincus's framework from [Life at the Speed of Play](https://www.lifeatthespeedofplay.com/).
Treat it as an evaluation lens, not a predictive model.
Its numerical rules are heuristics, not established success probabilities or required thresholds.
Do not use them to score ideas.

## Establish the purpose

Extract the target user, problem, current workaround, intended benefit, project purpose, and main constraints.
Reuse context already supplied.
Ask one focused question only when missing information could change the evaluation.
Otherwise, state reasonable assumptions and continue.

Describe the underlying need in one sentence without naming the proposed product.
Label this interpretation as a hypothesis.
Allow evidence to challenge both the need and the proposed solution.

Choose success criteria that fit the purpose:

- Personal tools: useful outcomes, time saved, learning value, and maintenance effort.
- Internal projects: workflow improvement, adoption, integration, and ownership.
- Open-source projects: user value, contributor effort, distribution, and maintenance.
- Commercial apps: adoption, willingness to pay, acquisition, retention where relevant, and operating costs.

Distinguish users from buyers when they differ.
Do not impose growth, monetization, or daily engagement requirements on every project.

## Research alternatives

Use available web search and page-reading tools to check current claims.
Search by category, user problem, and known alternatives where useful.
Include direct competitors, adjacent products, existing features, scripts, spreadsheets, manual work, and doing nothing.
Treat claims of uniqueness as hypotheses to investigate.

Choose the primary comparison by user, problem, and usage-context fit.
Explain the match using evidence, not product fame or revenue.
Distinguish a product's marketed audience from verified users.
If no close comparison emerges, report that limit without declaring the idea unique or doomed.

Inspect the strongest comparison in depth.
Use secondary comparisons only when they clarify a distinct mechanism or tradeoff.
Review relevant dimensions:

- How users discover the product and reach the first useful result.
- The main task, repeated use, and reasons to return when relevant.
- Pricing, payment, and operating costs when relevant.
- Distribution, access, integration, and switching effort.
- Data requirements, permissions, and dependencies.
- Maintenance, support, and any benefits that accumulate through use.

Record important omissions in the pitch as open decisions, not automatic requirements to copy.
Mark irrelevant dimensions as not applicable.
Check discontinued alternatives and removed features when evidence is available.
Separate documented failure causes from speculation.
If none emerge, report "none found in this search."

Prefer official documentation, pricing pages, changelogs, and firsthand accounts for factual claims.
Use reviews for reported experiences and possible problems, not representative demand estimates.
Cite material claims beside the finding and record the research date.
Product existence proves neither adoption nor the effectiveness of each feature.

Stop when further searches add no decision-relevant evidence.
State unresolved questions and source limits rather than repeating the search indefinitely.
If browsing fails or is unavailable, continue with an explicitly provisional assessment.
Separate user-supplied evidence from unverified prior knowledge.
Never present remembered pricing, audiences, or product behavior as current verified facts.

## Map Proven, Better, and New

Classify meaningful parts of the idea, including important assumptions the pitch omits.
Keep classification separate from evidence strength.
Split mixed elements when useful, or explain their overlap.
Leave unsupported classifications unresolved rather than forcing certainty.

| Category | Evaluation rule |
| --- | --- |
| Proven | Identify existing patterns and evidence of useful outcomes in a comparable setting. Mark observed patterns without outcome evidence as unconfirmed. |
| Better | Identify a specific improvement against the user's current alternative. Treat it as proposed until relevant behavior or measurements support it. |
| New | Identify an approach or assumption without adequate supporting evidence. Explain what depends on it and how failure affects the remaining value. |

For Proven, explain why a pattern transfers before suggesting reuse.
Do not require exact copies of interfaces, pricing, or engagement tactics.
For Better, account for switching effort and tradeoffs, not just added features or stated enthusiasm.
For New, assess critical dependencies rather than estimating a percentage of product novelty.

Label material statements as evidence, inference, or unknown where the distinction affects the decision.
Explain confidence through source quality, relevance, and missing information.
Do not invent numeric confidence scores.

Surface an adjacent approach when research supports one that could change the evaluation.
Explain which need it serves and how it differs from the proposal.
Do not force an alternative merely to fill the report.

## Identify risks and useful tests

Separate research findings from validation of this specific solution.
Complaints suggest possible improvements; they do not establish willingness to switch or pay.
Discount praise from friends and peers without treating their relationships as evidence against the idea.
Relevant observed behavior matters, including the creator's repeated use of a personal tool.

Ignore sunk spending as a reason to continue.
Account for reusable assets, future costs, and remaining commitments when they affect the decision.
Treat creator confidence as a belief to examine, not evidence of demand.

Check feasibility risks that could change the result.
Consider data access, platform restrictions, integration effort, privacy requirements, operating costs, and maintenance ownership where relevant.
Tie each risk to the idea; label unsupported concerns as assumptions rather than sourced findings.

For the most important unknowns, propose the smallest useful test.
Name the assumption, relevant participant or setting, observable result, and decision threshold.
Label suggested thresholds as proposals and explain their relevance to the project goal.
State what each test cannot establish, such as long-term retention from a short trial.
Suggest tests without contacting people, spending money, or running experiments unless separately authorized.
Keep tests brief; do not expand the evaluation into an implementation roadmap.

## Return an evaluation card

Scale the report to the idea and available evidence.
Use only distinct, decision-relevant findings; impose no minimum bullet count.
Do not claim the idea is validated by market research alone.

Include:

- Idea, purpose, target user, success criteria, and underlying need hypothesis.
- Research date, scope, important assumptions, and limitations.
- Closest alternatives and the reason for choosing the primary comparison.
- Proven, Better, and New findings with nearby sources, uncertainty, and relevant tradeoffs.
- Practical constraints and assumptions that could invalidate the proposal.
- Failed alternatives and documented lessons, or the limits of that search.
- Small tests for the most consequential unknowns.
- Strongest reasons to pursue or reconsider the idea, prioritized by likely decision impact.

When asked for advice, provide a conditional recommendation and explain what evidence would change it.
Avoid unsupported verdicts and numeric scores.
The user retains the decision.
